# Sourcing brassware from Muradabad at scale: a buyer's guide

Muradabad — "Pital Nagri", the brass city of Uttar Pradesh — has cast and engraved brass since the
Mughal era. Today the district produces well over a billion dollars of brass exports a year and supports
roughly a quarter of a million artisans across casting, polishing, engraving and finishing. For an
African importer, it is the single best place in the world to source character brassware at volume. It
is also a place where the wrong sourcing approach costs you money. Here is how to buy from it well.

## What Muradabad actually makes

- **Cast & solid brassware:** pots (surahi, lota), vases, idols and figurines, oil lamps and diyas.
- **Decorative & tableware:** trays, bowls, platters, candle holders, T-light stands.
- **Metal art:** wall art, sculptures, planters, large installations (sculpture-grade decor for
  hospitality and luxury retail).
- **Finishes:** antique, mirror-polish, patina, electroplated (gold/silver/nickel) — usually 7+ standard
  options, plus bespoke patinas.

## Why density is the advantage

Muradabad's real edge is geographic density. Inside a single neighbourhood you have the casting foundry,
the polishing unit, the engraver, the antique-finishing specialist and the packaging house — all within
walking distance. A complete supply chain inside a postcode. This is why the city can run a 200-piece
boutique batch and a 12,000-piece chain-retail order from the same network without losing the handcraft
character. **The craft scales here; in most places it doesn't.**

## How to specify brass so you get what you expect

Brass quality is mostly about three things — get them on the PO:

1. **Alloy & weight.** Specify the alloy (commonly a 70/30 copper-zinc) and the **net weight per piece**.
   Weight is the honest proxy for "solid vs thin". Thin-walled pieces are cheaper and feel it on the
   shelf. State a target weight tolerance.
2. **Finish.** Name the finish exactly (e.g. "antique patina, lacquered" vs "mirror polish, lacquered").
   Lacquer matters for African humidity — it slows tarnish. For unlacquered "living finish", say so.
3. **Casting vs spinning vs sheet.** Cast pieces (idols, animals) differ from spun/sheet pieces (trays,
   vases) in cost and look. Match the method to the product.

## MOQ and lead time reality

- **MOQ** on cast brassware commonly starts at **100–200 units**; decorative lines around 200–500.
- **Lead time** typically **21–35 days** from PO + sample approval, longer for heavy engraving (the most
  labour-intensive step) or large installations.
- **Sample first.** Always approve a physical sample before the order is cut. A good exporter couriers
  samples and locks the spec to that sample.

## Five red flags when vetting a supplier

1. They won't **name the foundry** or let you visit / see production photos.
2. They quote a bare price with **no net weight** specified.
3. No **pre-shipment QC** or inspection offered on container orders.
4. Vague on **lacquer / anti-tarnish** treatment for export humidity.
5. No **export-grade packaging** plan for the 35-day ocean haul (brass dents and tarnishes if packed for
   a showroom shelf, not a container).

## Four green flags

1. Factory named on the PO; visits and weekly production photos welcomed.
2. Net weight and alloy on the specification sheet.
3. Sampling + pre-shipment inspection standard on orders over ~1 CBM.
4. Anti-rust/anti-tarnish treatment and four-stage export packaging as routine.

## Packing brass for Africa

Brass is heavy, dents easily and tarnishes in transit humidity. Export-grade handling means: individual
tissue/foam wrap, anti-tarnish treatment, 5-ply inner and 7-ply outer cartons, humidity sachets per CBM,
and block-and-brace loading. Done right, damage stays under half a percent even over a 35-day haul and
multiple port handovers.

## The takeaway

Buy from Muradabad on **specification, not just price**: alloy, net weight, named finish, sample
approval and a real QC + packaging plan. Use the city's density to mix categories and finishes into one
container. Done this way, Muradabad gives an African retailer or hotel buyer a brass range that defends
its margin on the shelf — which is the whole point of sourcing character goods.
