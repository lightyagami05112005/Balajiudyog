# Why African importers are quietly shifting hardware sourcing to India

For two decades, "import hardware" meant "import from China" across most of Africa. That is changing —
not loudly, but consistently. Talk to a building-hardware distributor in Lagos, a hospitality buyer in
Nairobi or a furniture-fittings importer in Dar es Salaam, and the same reasons come up. None of them is
about price alone. They are about **risk, fit and margin.**

## 1. MOQs that match an African shelf, not a Chinese factory line

The single most common complaint we hear from African buyers about Chinese supply is the minimum order
quantity. A Chinese factory geared for container-scale runs often wants 3,000–10,000 units per SKU
before it will quote a good price. That forces an importer to over-buy a single design and tie up
working capital in slow-moving stock.

India's craft-and-engineering hubs are built differently. Because production is distributed across many
mid-sized units, **MOQs commonly start at 100–500 units per SKU**, and a buyer can spread volume across
categories to reach a full container. You order to your sell-through, not to the factory's batch size.
For a distributor carrying 200 SKUs across a showroom, that flexibility is worth more than a 3% price
difference.

## 2. Batch-to-batch consistency

The second recurring theme is consistency. A finish that drifts between orders, a hinge whose tolerance
changes shipment to shipment — these quietly cost a distributor returns and reputation. India's
multi-generational units, working to a documented quality system, tend to hold finish and tolerance
across reorders. The same engraver runs your 200-piece sample batch and your 12,000-piece reorder. For a
joinery shop or a retail chain standardising on a product, **predictability beats novelty.**

## 3. Documentation that clears the port

This is the unglamorous reason that decides repeat business. A container that sits at Apapa or Mombasa
because the conformity paperwork was wrong costs demurrage, storage and lost sell-through. Indian export
houses that serve Africa have built their back offices around **SONCAP (Nigeria), KEBS-PVoC (Kenya),
SABS (South Africa), GSA (Ghana) and TBS (Tanzania)** — validating documents to the destination port's
format before the container leaves Mundra. English-language trade desks and familiarity with African
customs behaviour remove a whole category of delay.

## 4. The heritage aesthetic holds margin

In mid-to-luxury retail and hospitality, the *look* matters. Hand-finished brass, real cut glass and
character hardware command a price that anonymous mass product cannot. African buyers serving rising
middle-class retail and a growing hotel sector increasingly want that aesthetic — and it is exactly what
India's Muradabad (brass), Firozabad (glass) and Aligarh (hardware) clusters do best. The product
defends its own margin on the shelf.

## 5. Mixed containers from one supplier

Perhaps the most underrated advantage: an Indian export house can consolidate brassware, locks, bathroom
hardware and glassware from different hubs into **one container, one bill of lading, one invoice.** Your
back office handles one supplier; your customs handles one shipment. For an importer running several
categories, that simplification — fewer POs, fewer B/Ls, fewer documentation errors — is a real
operating saving.

## What this means for the next 18 months

India's hardware and decor exports to Sub-Saharan Africa have outpaced the regional average for several
consecutive quarters. The shift is structural, not a price blip: it is driven by MOQ flexibility,
consistency, documentation discipline and aesthetic fit. Price parity with China is close enough that
these operating factors now decide the order.

The practical takeaway for an African importer: **run a single mixed container as a trial.** Approve the
samples first, put the factory name on the PO, and judge the supplier on the three things that actually
cost you money downstream — consistency, documentation, and on-time, undamaged delivery.
